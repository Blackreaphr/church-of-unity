This page explains why Catholic Bibles and the King James Version differ, what those differences are, and how to read wisely. The goal is careful reading across traditions: compare sources, notice translation choices, and think deeply with charity.

Examples are brief and representative. Check full contexts and edition notes.

Canon: Which Books Are Included?

Catholic Bibles include seven books called the Deuterocanon (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1 Maccabees, 2 Maccabees) and additions to Esther and Daniel (for example, the Prayer of Azariah and Susanna). The 1611 KJV printed these as the Apocrypha between the Testaments. Many later Protestant printings omitted them.

Sources and Translation

  • KJV (1611): Primarily from the Hebrew Masoretic Text (OT) and the Greek Textus Receptus (NT). Style is formal with relatively literal renderings.
  • Catholic lines: Older Douay-Rheims drew from the Latin Vulgate. Modern Catholic editions (for example, NABRE) translate from Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek manuscripts and include the Deuterocanon.

Different source texts and translation approaches account for many wording differences.

Examples: Wording and Content

Luke 1:28

KJV: "Hail, thou that art highly favoured."

Catholic (for example, NABRE): "Hail, full of grace."

Why it matters: The Greek permits both senses. The choice shapes how readers hear Maryâ€™s role. Compare notes and context instead of relying on a single English phrase.

Genesis 3:15

KJV: "it shall bruise thy head."

Douay-Rheims (Vulgate line): "she shall crush thy head."

Many modern editions: "he will strike" or "he will crush."

Why it matters: These readings reflect different manuscript traditions and interpretive histories. Theology often flows from translation choices. Reading more than one edition helps.

Tobit 12:9 and Sirach 3:30

Catholic: "Almsgiving delivers" or "almsgiving atones." Read within a full-Bible view of mercy and repentance.

KJV: In 1611 these verses appear in the Apocrypha. Many later KJV printings omit that section. Whether a Bible includes the Deuterocanon explains why a citation is present or absent.

Daniel additions

Catholic Bibles include the Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three Holy Children (Daniel 3), Susanna (Daniel 13), and Bel and the Dragon (Daniel 14). The 1611 KJV placed these in the Apocrypha section.

Why Knowing the Difference Helps

  • Honest comparison: See where a teaching rests on a particular manuscript family or canon choice.
  • Wider reading: The Deuterocanon preserves prayers, wisdom, and history that shaped early Christian reflection.
  • Better conversations: Naming sources calmly lowers heat and increases light.

Philosophy: Read with Common Sense

Respond with care. Verify claims, read closely, and compare sources. Ask three questions: Which text base is used? Which canon is assumed? What is the translator trying to make clear? Look for the strongest case on each side, then judge with reason and charity.

  • Three checks: context, cross-translation comparison, and a trusted commentary.
  • Two questions: What does this reveal about God? What fruit does it bear in life?
